It was half past two in the morning and I still had three hundred kilometres of Germany ahead of me. It was raining, the wipers were on their slowest setting, the radio was playing a Polish station I could not understand a word of. Two red lights ahead of me, belonging to a truck I had given up on overtaking twenty minutes earlier. Absolutely nothing was happening.

That was exactly why I was sitting there.

Describe this to someone who has never played these games and it sounds like a precise definition of boredom. You sit at a computer and do a job nobody pays you for, in a world where nothing chases you and nothing explodes. And yet Euro Truck Simulator 2 is still alive fourteen years later, with American Truck Simulator right beside it. They did not survive by being exciting. They survived because they offer something most games do not.

## A game where nothing happens

Most games treat your time as something to compress. The trip from A to B is the boring part, so it gets skipped, shortened, replaced by fast travel. ETS2 does the opposite. The drive **is not the obstacle between the content, the drive is the content**. Munich to Hamburg takes exactly as long as it should take, and the game never apologises for it.

Which leads to the second thing: there is no way to lose. You can be late, you can wreck the trailer, you can fall asleep at a rest stop and lose money. None of that is punishment in the way games usually mean it. No red text, no mission restart. You pay the fine and keep going, exactly like in real life. The game does not want performance from you. It just wants you to arrive.

And then there is the geography. It hit me the first time in ATS, driving through a town I had never visited but knew from photos. Then in ETS2 near Žilina, where I recognised the exit. It is a strange feeling no ordinary game can manufacture: the game reaches outside itself and grabs a piece of reality you already own. It has happened to me many times since and it has never worn off.

## The wheel I had to bolt down every time

It was not the game that got me into simulators. It was the wheel.

A Logitech Driving Force GT. My parents bought it for me when I was small, and it was definitely not an investment into sim racing, it was simply a gift. Nine hundred degrees of rotation, force feedback, two plastic pedals that slid forward across the carpet at the worst possible moment every time. I remember the ritual better than I remember most games from that period: pull it out of the box, clamp it to the desk, tighten it, run the cable, position the pedals. Then the whole thing in reverse afterwards, because the desk was also where I did homework and the wheel could not stay there.

And it had no clutch. Two pedals, throttle and brake. I was holding a piece of plastic that did a fairly convincing impression of a truck, and shifting gears like in an arcade game.

That taught me the first thing I take away from all of this. **Immersion is not a scale from zero to one hundred, it is a set of details, and one missing detail is louder than ten present ones.** The wheel gave me the weight of the steering, the resistance in a corner, that small tug when the tyres bite on wet asphalt. And with every gear change it reminded me it was an imitation.

![The Logitech Driving Force GT, my first wheel](/images/articles/465848/volant.jpg)

### The mouse that took my head away

So I left the wheel behind. Not because it was bad, but because ten minutes of setup before a drive and ten minutes after is a tax you pay twice, maybe three times, and after that you simply stop launching the game.

Mouse steering is surprisingly precise. The truck goes where you drag it, and at motorway speed you can hold a lane with one finger. The problem was somewhere else. The mouse has two jobs in that game and you only get to give it one. Steer with it and you lose the ability to look around. My head was stuck facing forward like I was wearing a neck brace. Holding right click let me look, but the moment I did, the truck stopped responding to steering.

In a game where half the work is checking mirrors and glancing out of the left window, that was a disaster. A roundabout could be taken blind. Reversing a trailer to a dock could not be done at all, because you were either turning or seeing, never both. I ended up living in the external cameras, and that is precisely where the reason I was sitting there in the first place quietly evaporated.

### The controller as the middle path

And then came the controller, and the problem was gone.

An analogue stick has enough resolution to hold a line at a hundred and thirty without weaving across the lane. The right stick finally freed my head, so I look around independently of steering, exactly like in a car. The triggers are pressure sensitive, so the throttle is not just on or off. And best of all: you finish, you drop it in a drawer, done. Setup time zero.

From that moment on I was an unstoppable machine. The distance I have covered with a controller is a multiple of everything I ever drove on the wheel, and not because the controller is more faithful. It is not. It is because the best input device is not the most accurate one, it is the one with the least friction between "I want to drive" and "I am driving". The wheel gave me one hundred percent of the feeling in exchange for a ritual. The controller gives me eighty and asks for nothing.

## The community that keeps writing the game

No game survives fourteen years on its own. ETS2 survived because SCS Software opened the doors and the community walked in and started rebuilding.

Map mods that finished Europe in places the official DLCs had not reached yet. Reworked physics and engine sounds recorded from real vehicles. Trucks from brands the studio will never get a licence for. Thousands of skins, trailers, cabin accessories, traffic signs, weather packs. An entire ecosystem of virtual trucking companies with their own rules, convoys and websites. People who turned a game about driving into a second job, voluntarily and for free.

One thing about this fascinates me. Most games defend themselves against being rewritten by the player. SCS did the exact opposite: it built a world, gave it formats that can be opened, and handed the keys out. The result is that when something is missing from the game, the community usually does not respond with a complaint. It responds by making the thing.

Which is exactly what happened to me.

## Yellow Express, or bringing your own street into the game

There is a company based near where I live called Yellow Express. I saw their fleet constantly, on the way to school, at the shop, on the motorway. They were part of what my surroundings looked like.

They were not in the game. And suddenly I had the tools to change that.

The first skin I ever made was about precisely this. I hunted down photos from every angle, because the logo on the door sits differently than the logo on the trailer and I wanted both. The colour never matched on the first attempt, or the fifth. I redrew, exported, launched the game, discovered that the lettering on the side ended exactly where the geometry breaks the texture, and went back again. Nobody asked me to do it and nobody except me was ever going to see it.

And then I pulled out of the depot with a trailer that looked exactly like the one that had overtaken me on the bypass that morning.

> That was the moment the game stopped being a place I go to escape to, and became a place I bring my own things into.

## DriveWay Group a.s.

One skin turned into more, naturally. My friend and I stopped recreating other people's companies and started our own: [DriveWay Group a.s.](https://x.com/drivewaygroupas)

It was more work than it looks, and most of it had nothing to do with driving. A logo that has to work on a door and across an entire trailer. A consistent palette across the trucks, so a group screenshot looks like a fleet and not like a collection. An account where we posted photos from our runs. Rules for how we drive in convoy. All that surrounding world that is written down nowhere in the game and that you feel anyway.

![The beginning of the DriveWay Group](/images/articles/465848/drivewayzaciatok.jpg)

![A convoy from one of our runs together](/images/articles/465848/drivewaykonvoj.jpg)

The project eventually faded. Not because we fell out, and not because we stopped enjoying it. School happened, other things happened, other projects happened, and the drives thinned out from every evening to occasionally to never. That is how these things tend to go and I do not think of it as a loss.

I think of it as left open. I have learned enough since then to bring things to it we did not have back then, and I can picture us putting our forces together again and building something that is not just another virtual company with a nice logo.

## Why I found quiet in it

Back to that opening paragraph, back to half past two in the morning.

Most things want my attention in fragments now. A notification, a video, a message, another video. Attention gets torn into pieces and none of them is big enough to finish even a single thought inside. Driving in ETS2 asks for the exact opposite: one continuous, low intensity kind of attention. You have to be present, because the road keeps changing, but you do not have to be tense.

And in that space, thinking becomes possible. A good portion of the things I have ever sorted out in my head got sorted out somewhere between Cologne and Rotterdam.

The second reason is entirely practical. A job has a clear beginning and a clear end. Load, drive, unload, get paid. Life hands you surprisingly few tasks like that. Most of what I do is in progress, never finished, and always improvable. Here it is done when the last message fades from the screen, and nobody comes asking for another iteration.

I do not want to romanticise it more than it deserves, though. There were stretches where I launched it specifically so I would not have to think about something, and that is not quiet, that is running away. From the outside the two look identical. Only I can tell the difference, and even I cannot always tell it straight away.

> ETS2 is not a game about trucks. It is a game about having a destination, a road to it, and nobody rushing you.

## What is still ahead

I am writing this, fittingly, on the day Gamescom opens in Cologne, where SCS Software has its biggest booth so far and is bringing playable builds of several upcoming maps.

For ETS2 that means Iceland, probably the strangest direction a European map could have taken. No motorway network, no dense cluster of cities, just one road around an island, glaciers and wind. For ATS, British Columbia and South Dakota are playable, North Dakota was officially announced in August, and Indiana is somewhere in the pipeline. Alongside all of it runs Coaches, meaning buses, which will change the game more than it sounds: a passenger is not a pallet, stops are in different places than warehouses, and the whole rhythm of a drive moves somewhere else.

But that is just what you can copy off a blog. The more interesting part is what nobody has done yet.

We still do not have an economy that genuinely reacts to you, where demand for something dries up because you were the one hauling it. Weather is still scenery rather than an opponent. Running your own company is a table of numbers, when it could be the most interesting layer in the whole game. And the part that pulls at me most is what happens outside the game: telemetry streams out in real time and you can build almost anything on top of it, from your own dashboard on a second monitor to a system that logs your runs by itself. I am working on the second one right now, and it is funny how far it takes you from the original question, which was driving.

So I will probably end up back where I started, just with a controller in my hand instead of a wheel bolted to a desk. The game has changed less over the years than you might expect. I have changed quite a bit next to it.

When was the last time you pulled into a rest stop in ETS2 or ATS for no reason at all and left the engine running?
