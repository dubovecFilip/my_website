This is not an article about how great it is. It is about **how it came to be**. Step by step, with the decisions I had to make along the way, including the ones I got wrong and had to redo.

If you only care about the result, skip to the end. If you care how a thing like this gets built, come along from the start.

## Day zero: an invalidated delivery

I drove a few hundred honest kilometres, delivered, and the service that was supposed to log it **threw the delivery out**.

The reason? I had lane assist switched on.

Not cruise control at 150. Not a teleport. **An assist that ships with the game.**

I sat there for a while and two things landed. First, that somebody else was deciding whether my drive counted. Second, and worse: **I did not have the data at all**. It lives on someone else's server, under rules that can change tomorrow.

So I opened an editor.

## Decision 1: the ideology first, the code second

The first thing that existed in this project was not code. It was a document.

I wrote down **what the thing should do and what it must never do**. That sounds like pointless ceremony for a hobby project, but it saved me a lot of arguing with myself later.

The main sentence reads like this and still sits at the top of the README:

> **A delivery is never invalidated because the driver used an assist.**

That also settled what **does** get recorded: assists, cruise control, speeding, collisions, fines. All of it. But as **metadata**, never as grounds for refusal.

The difference between "I will record this" and "this will punish you" is the whole project.

## Decision 2: local, or it is not worth doing

The second decision followed immediately. **No account, no server, no internet.**

The database is plain SQLite on my disk. You can open it with anything. Copy it to a stick. Nobody can take it away.

I knew what I was giving up. Nothing can be shared, there are no leaderboards, there is no company feed. And I knew what I was buying: **if I shut the project down tomorrow, you lose nothing.**

That seemed like the better trade.

## Decision 3: a logbook for the player, not the career

This one is subtler, but it mattered.

The games let you have several profiles and several careers. I spent a while wondering whether to keep them apart, whether each career should have its own history.

In the end, no. **Waybill is the player's logbook**, not the career's. I do not care which profile you drove it on. You drove it.

It simplified the data model and, more importantly, it matches how a person actually thinks about it.

## Getting at the data at all

SCS games have telemetry, but not for free. You need a **plugin** that pushes the data out through shared memory. I used RenCloud's, which is MIT licensed.

To make it easier, the plugin installs from inside the app. *Play → Install telemetry plugin*, pick the `.dll`, done. That is a triviality, but skip it and the first step for a new user is "find a file on a forum and copy it into the right folder", and half of them are gone right there.

After that it is just reading. **Ten times a second**, so no event can slip between two samples.

## The first real problem: what is a job

It sounds silly until you try to program it.

The game never says "a job has started, a job has ended". It reports state. You have to infer the transitions.

I built a state machine with three states: **idle, accepted, driving**. And for each job a fingerprint, a combination of cities, companies, cargo, income and deadline. From that I can tell whether this is still the same job or a different one.

That fingerprint paid off somewhere else entirely: **when the app dies mid drive**, the restart uses it to recognise that the unfinished job on disk is the one the game is reporting right now, and carries on inside it.

## The second problem: the game remembers events forever

This one cost me the most nerves and is a lovely example of how telemetry can be misread.

The data has a block holding the last fine, the last delivery, the last ferry. Except it **stays there forever**. You deliver a job and an hour later it still says you delivered, with the numbers from the old one.

I saw it beautifully in a recording: on one sample in the middle of a run to Page, it was showing a delivery of 172 km. That was the **previous job**, finished two hours earlier.

Read that naively and your app logs one delivery twenty times.

The decision: **on ordinary samples I ignore those values entirely.** An event only counts when the game announces it separately, and then I write a dedicated line tagged with its name. No "the flag is set, so it probably happened".

## The third problem: what units am I even measuring in

This is my favourite part, because it is completely counterintuitive.

These games have **two different worlds**:

* **simulated kilometres**, which the odometer counts, and which the job offer and the payout are in
* **actual position on the map**, which is compressed by roughly **13.5 times**

Measure distance from movement across the map and a three hundred kilometre haul comes out as twenty. Mix the two and you get nonsense.

The decision: **distance comes from the odometer.** That is the same unit the job offer, the payout and the delivery screen all use. World position is stored **separately**, for teleport detection and one day for the map.

And right behind it came another trap. I wanted average speed, so I divided distance by time. Nonsense again.

Why? Because **game time runs twenty times faster**, and if you sleep in the cab it jumps nine hours forward. One drive that really took me 21 minutes stretched to 19 game hours.

The decision: the denominator is **time spent driving**, not time from start to finish. Sleep, pauses and ferries come out of it.

## Decision 4: three states and only three grounds for refusal

Once I had the data, the question was what to do with it.

I landed on three states:

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `accepted` | nothing unusual was found |
| `review` | something is worth a look, but nothing suggests cheating |
| `rejected` | direct evidence the drive did not happen |

And here is the heart of the project. **Rejection is reserved for one thing: claiming a delivery you did not drive.** There are exactly three such cases:

* a teleport across the map that no vehicle could have driven
* an odometer that moved further in one instant than driving can account for
* essentially zero distance

Everything else is a **flag**. The delivery keeps its distance, its payout and its place in the statistics. The statistics count **every** row, whatever its state.

And one more thing I care about: **not finishing a delivery is not cheating.** You switch profiles, you quit the game, it crashes. None of that claims anything, so there is nothing to refuse. It gets written down as an unfinished drive, with the kilometres kept.

## Decision 5: keep the raw recordings

This decision looks like waste and is the most important one in the project.

Alongside the database of **conclusions**, Waybill keeps the **raw recordings**. Every second of every drive exactly as the game reported it. Nothing interpreted.

Gzipped they are about thirteen times smaller, and they are never deleted.

Why bother? Because **the rules of judgement change**. And when you fix them, old rows would carry the verdict of whatever version was current that day, forever. With the recordings it takes one command:

```bash
Waybill.exe --rebuild
```

and the whole history is judged again.

**Conclusions are an opinion. The recording is evidence.** You do not throw evidence away.

Later I trimmed the line down. I went looking at what was actually in it and found every speed stored **three times** (metres per second, km/h and mph), every game time twice (as minutes and as a date), and cabin camera geometry nobody reads. All of it derived, all of it removable without losing anything.

## And then reality started arguing

This is where it turned from "I am writing an app" into "the app is telling me things and I have to work out whether it is right".

In a single day it accused me three times.

### The teleport that was a loaded save

Provo to Price, rejected. Apparently a **jump across the map**.

I had not jumped anywhere. I loaded an older save.

The game clock runs backwards, the position moves, and in raw data it looks like the fraud of the century.

The decision: a clock running backwards is the **one thing** that means a save was loaded. Since then the app recognises it and counts nothing from that moment.

### The unstable client that was a pause

Winslow to Las Vegas went into review because the app had apparently **stopped measuring three times**. Holes of 44, 29 and 59 seconds.

I nearly blamed it on the machine being busy with something else. Then I checked how far the game clock had moved across those holes.

**By one minute.** Every time. At the twenty times rate it should have been fifteen.

So the game was not running. I was in a menu.

The bug was in the rule: it compared game time against **real** time. But game time is published in whole minutes, so over a short pause it reads zero or one depending on whether a minute boundary happened to fall inside. **A lottery, not a measurement.**

I rewrote it to compare against the rate the game **reports for itself** (which the app had never read, despite having it right there). The result surprised me: **not one of the nine so called stalls in my whole history was a stall.** Every one was a pause.

And it gave me a rule I now use everywhere: **when you cannot tell, decide for the driver.** Being unable to prove the app stalled is not evidence that it did.

### The sleep that was loading

Third time. A delivery claimed I had taken a **25 minute nap**. I had not.

I opened the recording and there it was, second by second:

```
18:35:23   clock 10673   loaded = no
18:35:29   clock 10699   loaded = yes
```

The game was **loading the cargo** and skipped 25 minutes of game time doing it. Exactly like a sleep. The only difference is that at that moment the cargo changed hands.

All three fixes ended the same way. One command and **the whole history was recomputed**. Not redriven. Recomputed.

That is precisely what the recordings are for.

## What fell out of the data once I looked properly

This is the fun part. When you have raw data, things fall out of it you would never otherwise learn.

### A triple is not one damage figure

In the game a trailer has **one number**, even when you are pulling three.

Telemetry has them separately. I looked at one of my triples:

| | body | chassis | wheels |
|---|---|---|---|
| unit 1 | 0.547 % | 0.684 % | 3.305 % |
| unit 2 | 0.034 % | 0.042 % | 3.208 % |
| unit 3 | 0.034 % | 0.042 % | 3.205 % |

**The wheels wore evenly across all three.** That is ordinary wear from a long haul. But the body damage landed on **the first unit only**, and fifteen times over.

That is the difference between "I clipped something" and "I drove a long way". One number throws it away.

![A triple taken apart](/images/articles/714203/waybill-triple.png)

The decision: remember **every unit separately**, in the order it is hitched, with its own plate and its own damage. And tell a **dolly from a trailer**, because otherwise the average gets diluted by a converter that carries nothing.

A side note: a real box triple reports **five units**, three trailers and two dollies. And the game knows what the set is called, including things like `rmdouble`, a Rocky Mountain double.

### A deadline that does not exist

World of Trucks jobs claimed I had delivered them **four billion game minutes early**.

The game puts `4294967295` in that field. That is not a date. It is the largest number that fits, and it means "there is no deadline here". World of Trucks jobs have a real deadline, on the website, not in the game.

While I was at it I started storing **where a job came from**: quick job, cargo market, World of Trucks. The app had been getting it from the game all along and throwing it away.

## Decision 6: never lose data

This came from asking myself an uncomfortable question: *what does a rebuild do to a delivery whose recording is gone?*

The answer was: **it deletes it.** The rebuild threw everything out first and only then built it back.

When I looked into why, I found a better bug. A delivery's identifier was a **random number generated every time it was derived**. So the same drive had a different identity each run and nothing could be matched to anything. That is why it had to delete blindly.

Two fixes:

1. **Identity is computed from what makes it that delivery**: the game, the moment the job was accepted, and the offer itself. The same drive always comes out the same.
2. **A rebuild only touches the periods the recordings actually cover.** What it cannot restore, it leaves alone, and it tells you how much it left.

I tested it by adding an invented delivery from 2019 to a test database, one no recording covers. It survived.

## Decision 7: a window that does not shout

For a long time I used the app from the command line and was fine with it. But a logbook you cannot look into is not a logbook.

A few decisions I made about the window:

**Dark only.** Two themes means checking every control twice, and one of them is always the neglected one.

**Amber, not blue.** Blue is boring and goes muddy on a dark ground. Amber is the colour of indicators and warning boards, which suits trucks.

**Fixed column widths.** Narrow the window and I would rather have a horizontal scrollbar than fourteen columns squeezed into unreadable slivers.

**A verdict is never left unexplained.** I consider this the most important thing in the interface. A delivery's card opens with *Why this verdict*, where every flag is explained in a **sentence**, with the figures behind it where two measurements disagreed. And under it a line that is really a summary of the whole ideology:

*"None of this refuses the delivery. It counts in full, distance and payout included."*

![A delivery on its card](/images/articles/714203/waybill-card.png)

**The timeline slides out, it does not sit there.** When nothing happened along the way it was taking a third of the card for nothing. Now it comes out from the side when you ask for it.

![The timeline](/images/articles/714203/waybill-timeline.png)

**A dot for decimals, not a comma.** A small thing, but when the window says `283,0 km` and the docs and the exports say `283.0 km`, one day you read it wrong.

## Decision 8: Discord over the local pipe

I wanted my profile to show where I am heading.

I could have done it with a webhook or a bot. I chose **Rich Presence over the local pipe**, because it is the only one of those that **sends nothing anywhere**. Discord runs on the same machine and the app talks to it directly.

No account, no server, and if Discord is not running then nothing shows up.

## When I found out it already exists

Somewhere in the middle of this I ran into TrucksLog and VTLog and felt uncomfortable for a while.

Then I looked properly. They are **web services**. Mandatory account, someone else's server, premium tiers, server side anti-cheat.

And it hit me that they are not competition, they are **a different category**. They have to be online, because their business model rests on it. I do not have to be online at all.

The decision: **do not chase their feature list.** Build what follows from my own approach instead, the things they cannot do.

## What is next

**The map and route replay.** I have been recording coordinates since the very first drive, even though nothing draws them yet. So the data is already there.

**Statistics per period.** Best month, longest session, that particular joy of competing with yourself.

**Signing the recordings.** Small, local, no server. But it is the foundation for anything later: when the client holds the raw record, a delivery can be **proved** rather than approved by somebody. That is the thing server side services cannot do.

And somewhere far back in my head, a home server. But on one condition only: **the local version has to work without it, forever.** The moment Waybill needs the internet to show you your own drives, it stops being the thing I wrote it to be.

## What it taught me

Three things I did not know before this project.

**Data is not truth, data is testimony.** The game never lies to you on purpose. It tells you what it sees, and the entire job is translating that into what actually happened.

**It pays to keep the evidence.** Three times I had to rewrite a rule, and three times I could recompute the past instead of redriving it.

**When you are not sure, decide for the person.** An app that would rather accuse you than admit it does not know is worse than an app that occasionally misses something.

Which is, incidentally, exactly what bothered me about the service that threw out my first delivery.

## One more thing

If you have ever written something similar, say something. I would mostly like to know one thing: **how many times your own program accused you of something you did not do.**

Mine managed three in a single day. And every time it was right about what it saw, and wrong about what it thought that meant.
